


* National and International Security Implications

As IoT devices become more widespread, their vulnerabilities can have significant consequences for national and international security. Compromised IoT devices can be used as tools for cyber warfare, espionage, and even sabotage. For example, in 2016, the Mirai botnet attack disrupted major websites and services, highlighting the potential for large-scale disruption caused by insecure IoT devices [cite:@koliasDDoSIoTMirai2017].
In this case, the attack was based on Internet connected video cameras that came configured with a default password.
Once in, the attackers created a network of `bots' from the large number of compromised cameras to launch distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks on the websites of particular companies.
Mirai is one notable example, but there have been many similar successful attacks on a variety of IoT systems. 

The IoT infrastructure, especially in critical sectors such as energy, transportation, and healthcare, is becoming an attractive target for nation-state actors and terrorists.
A cyber attack on these systems could have severe consequences, including loss of life and property, economic disruption, and geopolitical instability.

To safeguard national and international security, it is essential to address the security challenges in IoT and develop a resilient and secure ecosystem that can withstand potential threats.

* Why IoT devices are a concern

As pointed out by cite:@ahmadMachineLearningApproaches2021, there are a number of challenges that are specific to IoT, including:

- Low cost: IoT devices are expected to be inexpensive and security can be costly.
- Resource constraints: IoT devices may need to operate on battery power with limited memory and compute resources and thus may not be sufficiently capable to securely encrypt communication.
- Internet access: devices such as video cameras are often unprotected from firewalls.
- Software updates: IoT end-users may be reluctant to upgrade or unaware that their device's software is out of date.

It might be argued that IoT devices are often of little consequence. Does it matter if your fridge is hacked?
Apart from the potential for botnets as noted previously, insecure devices can affect the whole network that they belong to by effectively providing a `backdoor' route to get past firewalls.

* Centralized Architectures: A Single Point of Failure

Centralized architectures are currently the norm for IoT systems, which means that a central server controls all connected devices. While this structure simplifies management, it also creates a single point of security failure. If a hacker compromises the central server, they can potentially gain control of all the IoT devices connected to it. This vulnerability highlights the need for a more secure and decentralized approach to IoT management.



One notorious example of this vulnerability was the Mirai botnet attack in 2016. The Mirai malware infected IoT devices, such as cameras and routers, turning them into a botnet that launched massive Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks against internet infrastructure company Dyn, causing widespread internet outages [cite:@koliasDDoSIoTMirai2017].

Citation: Kolias, C., Kambourakis, G., Stavrou, A., & Voas, J. (2017). DDoS in the IoT: Mirai and other botnets. Computer, 50(7), 80-84.

* Data Ownership in IoT

A critical question in IoT security is who owns the data generated by smart devices. Ideally, data producers (i.e., the device users) should be autonomous data owners. However, IoT manufacturers often end up with default ownership, raising concerns about privacy and potential misuse of sensitive information. Establishing clear guidelines for data ownership and control is essential for securing IoT ecosystems.

For example, the controversy around Amazon's Ring doorbell illustrates the issue of data ownership. Amazon faced backlash after it was discovered that the company shared user data and video footage with law enforcement without explicit consent from the users (Booth, 2020).

Citation: Booth, R. (2020). Amazon's Ring is the largest civilian surveillance network the US has ever seen. The Guardian.

* Managing Change of Ownership

Currently, there is no standard method for managing the change of ownership of IoT devices. This lack of protocol can lead to security risks as previous owners may retain access to the device, its data, or associated services. Implementing a standardized approach to securely transferring device ownership would help mitigate these risks.

In 2018, security researchers found that second-hand Nest thermostats could still be controlled by the original owner even after the device had been sold or given away, potentially allowing previous owners unauthorized access to the new owner's home (Newman, 2018).

Citation: Newman, L. H. (2018). Even after a factory reset, your old Nest thermostat might give you away. Wired.

* Autonomy and Productisation

The Arduino family of systems has greatly contributed to IoT development by making the technology more accessible. However, once a device is productized, its autonomy often disappears, leaving it at the mercy of the manufacturer or service provider. Ensuring that IoT devices maintain their autonomy even after productization is crucial for their security and user control.

One example is the controversy surrounding Sonos smart speakers, which became obsolete after a software update. Users found themselves unable to use their devices, forcing them to either upgrade or lose functionality (Lomas, 2020).

Citation: Lomas, N. (2020). Sonos clarifies how unsupported devices will be treated. TechCrunch.

* Broker Architecture: A Partial Solution

A broker architecture could be implemented to handle the question "who owns me" from IoT devices. In this setup, a broker would be responsible for managing device ownership information. However, this solution still presents a central point of failure, as the broker itself could be compromised.

In 2017, the Reaper botnet targeted IoT devices by exploiting vulnerabilities in centralized brokers and IoT device management systems, leading to a large-scale cyber attack (Chen, Luo, & Liu, 2018).

Citation: Chen, Y., Luo, X., & Liu, Q. (2018). A first look at IoT malware ecosystem in the wild. arXiv preprint arXiv:1809.07368.

* Blockchain: A Decentralized Approach

A blockchain-based ledger of IoT ownership could offer a more secure and decentralized solution. Blockchain technology inherently resists tampering and provides transparency, making it an ideal choice for managing device ownership and data sharing. However, the infrastructure cost of supporting a blockchain network remains a challenge. Incentivizing stakeholders to support the network and bear its costs will be vital for the adoption of blockchain-based solutions in IoT security.

A notable example of a blockchain-based IoT solution is IOTA, a distributed ledger designed specifically for IoT devices. IOTA uses a unique architecture called the Tangle, which enables secure data transfer and feeless transactions between IoT devices 

Citation: Popov, S. (2018). The Tangle. IOTA Whitepaper.

* Conclusion

As the Internet of Things continues to expand, addressing security issues becomes increasingly important. By exploring decentralized approaches like blockchain technology, we can build a more secure and user-centric IoT ecosystem that mitigates the risks associated with centralized architectures, clarifies data ownership, and enables secure change of device ownership. Embracing these solutions and learning from real-world examples will be key to unlocking the full potential of IoT while ensuring the privacy and safety of its users. With concerted efforts from industry stakeholders, policymakers, and researchers, we can work towards a future where IoT devices are both convenient and secure.
